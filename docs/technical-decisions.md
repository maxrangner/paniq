# Technical decisions

## Chosen foundation

| Area | Decision | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Engine | Unity 6.3 LTS (`6000.3.24f1`) | Supported Windows workflow, C#, mature 3D tools, and a future ECS path. |
| Rendering | URP | A scalable Unity rendering path suitable for desktop and later mobile quality profiles. |
| Initial runtime model | GameObjects and C# | Keeps the prototype approachable and fast to iterate. |
| Input | Unity Input System | Keeps device input separate from game intent. |
| Tests | Unity Test Framework | Supports edit-mode logic tests and play-mode scene-flow tests. |
| Version control | Git | Text Unity assets are tracked; LFS waits for large source media. |

## Prototype decision: fire-reaction bring-up

| Item | Decision | Why now | Revisit when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scenario data | One `FireReactionScenario` ScriptableObject with an explicit seed, stable agent IDs, and fixed spatial values | It gives the first prototype repeatable starting data without putting mutable run state in the Unity scene. [Unity ScriptableObject manual](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.3/Documentation/Manual/class-ScriptableObject.html) | The game needs multiple scenario variants or a designer-facing authoring workflow. |
| Simulation cadence | One `FixedUpdate` runner at the existing 0.02-second fixed step | The fire and agent rules advance in a predictable order even when the game renders at a different frame rate. [Unity fixed updates](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.3/Documentation/Manual/fixed-updates.html) | A replay-compatibility review approves a different fixed step. |
| Crowd movement (revised 2026-09-18) | Integer steering with whole-degree headings: agents turn and accelerate gradually toward a goal, blended with pushes away from people within 0.8 m and walls within 0.6 m. Occupancy checks and the rotating resolver are unchanged; the swept-circle test is now exact for moves in any direction. No Unity physics or navigation. | Owner playtest: four-direction movement read as robotic and panicked agents ran into walls and stayed there. Steering gives curved, eased motion while staying replayable. | A prototype stone needs obstacles or routes that local steering cannot handle; then consider AI Navigation. |
| Integer trigonometry | Fixed 91-entry sine table (scaled by 10,000), an integer binary-search heading function, and integer square root in `IntegerMath` | Free headings need sine and cosine, but floating-point results must not decide outcomes. A table is small, exact, and tested against `Math.Sin`. | Headings need finer than one-degree resolution. |
| Fire reaction and facing | Integer-only forward 90-degree, 3 m vision cone around the agent's current heading, tested against every burning cell | Seeing danger in front of an agent reads more clearly than an invisible radius. | A prototype stone needs occlusion, smoke, or sound direction. |
| Calm behaviour defaults | Per-agent walk pace from the speed trait, 20–32 mm/tick (1.0–1.6 m/s) ±2 (was seeded 22–30 before personalities), turn rate 4–7°/tick, acceleration 2 mm/tick²; decisions every 60–200 ticks between strolling, standing, glancing around, and standing near someone | Small independent decisions read as people rather than random motion; normal human walking pace is about 1.3 m/s. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | Playtesters say the crowd looks too busy, too idle, or too uniform. |
| Panic behaviour defaults | Sprint from the speed trait, 60–110 mm/tick (3–5.5 m/s) ±5 (was seeded 70–100 before personalities), turn rate 10–16°/tick, acceleration 8 mm/tick²; re-decide every 20–60 ticks by scoring 8 random escape spots; 35% chance of a 30–70° swerve; 12% chance of a 5–15 tick hesitation; follow nearby runners at 35% weight; flee directly within 1.5 m of fire; give up a blocked direction after 12 ticks | The owner asked for visible panic: faster running, zig-zags, and changing decisions mid-run. Measured in a headless run of the default seed: about 3.3 m/s average and about two sharp (>30°) direction changes per second in the first 20 s. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | Playtesters find panic unreadable or too twitchy; lower the swerve chance or lengthen the decision interval first. |
| Maximum step distance | Raised from 60 to 120 mm per tick | The panic sprint needs up to 100 mm per tick. It is still well under the 500 mm agent diameter, so swept contact remains meaningful. | Faster movement is needed. |
| Alert propagation (revised 2026-09-18) | A yell is a simulation "sound" with a position and two reaches: calm people within 2.5 m understand it and become alert with their own seeded reaction delay; calm people within 6 m only hear it and turn to look (`AgentNoticedSound`). Runners also shout every 2–5 s while fleeing. | The owner asked for sound as a system indication that makes people turn toward it. Two reaches let a yell spread attention further than alarm, without real audio, scene physics or object references. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | A prototype stone needs walls that block sound or real audio. |
| Alert expression (revised 2026-09-18) | Alerted listeners turn toward what alarmed them: visual alerts face the nearest burning point, yell and bump alerts face where the noise came from | A heard warning still does not pretend the listener can see the fire, but turning toward the yeller often lets them see it themselves. | The prototype needs richer memory. |
| Hearing defaults | Fire crackles within 3.5 m of the nearest burning square; a collision or trip thud carries 3 m. A calm person who hears a noise turns toward it for 1–2.5 s (`Investigating`), turning at their panic turn rate, and after 0.5 s edges toward it at half walking pace if it is over 2 m away. Only calm people react to noises. | Hearing reaches just past the 3 m sight range, so someone with their back to the fire turns around, finds nothing in sight at the edge of hearing, and creeps closer until they see it. Calm-only keeps the rules readable. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | Playtesters find the room too twitchy (shorten the reach) or fire too easy to miss (lengthen it). |
| Panic temperaments | Dealt like a shuffled deck, not rolled independently: 15% freeze for good, 30% freeze for 2–6 s (or until fire is within 1.5 m) then run, the rest run. Ten people: 2, 3 and 5. Only who gets which comes from the seed. | The owner asked for some people to freeze in fear, some briefly and some for good. Independent rolls left the default seed with no permanent freezer at all, so the owner might never see it. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | The mix feels wrong in play, or scenarios need authored per-person temperaments. |
| Collisions and falls | A fleeing runner blocked by an upright person runs into them when their closing speed is at least 50 mm/tick (2.5 m/s). At 100 mm/tick (5 m/s) or more both are knocked down for 1.2–2.8 s and take 0.5 s to get up; below that both stagger for 0.2–0.4 s with a 25–50° jolt. A runner blocked by someone on the floor steps around them if any side-step works and otherwise trips over them. Nobody ever overlaps: a collision is a move that did not happen, resolved after movement in the order it was recorded. | The owner asked for panic to feel frantic, with fast collisions putting both people on the floor. 5 m/s means any head-on pair of runners, or a top-speed sprinter into someone standing. Measured headless over seeds 40–46: 13–31 collisions and 4–12 knockdowns in the first 20 s. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | Playtesters find the floor too crowded (raise the knockdown speed) or collisions too rare (lower panic people-avoidance from 50%). |
| Tripping | A runner going at least 60 mm/tick (3 m/s) has a 4% chance per panic decision to trip, doubled while zig-zagging; down for 0.8–2 s. Tripping over a fallen person uses the same fall time. | The owner asked that erratic panicking people sometimes trip. An earlier draft tripped runners over anyone on the floor even when they could step around, which gave up to 28 trips in 20 s; stepping around first brought it to 2–12. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | Trips look too common or too rare in play. |
| Icon drawing | Icons are `LineRenderer` shapes and bold `TextMesh` text on a per-person anchor that always faces the camera, drawn with the built-in unlit `Sprites/Default` shader so colours stay bright and can fade. | Icons were children of the capsule, so they spun with it and would tip over when it fell. Line shapes can draw the arcs and snowflake the owner asked for without adding art assets or packages. [LineRenderer](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.3/Documentation/ScriptReference/LineRenderer.html) | Icons need authored art. `Sprites/Default` is already in **Project Settings > Graphics > Always Included Shaders**, so standalone builds keep it; the code falls back to URP Unlit if it is ever removed. |
| Prototype fire shape (revised 2026-09-18) | A 24 × 24 grid of 500 mm cells. The fire starts in one seeded cell; each burning cell lights one random unburnt 4-neighbour every 40–120 ticks and never goes out. Each ignition is a `FireSpread` event caused by the cell that lit it. Shown as a glowing tile plus 2–3 small animated cubes per cell. | The owner asked for fire made of small animated cubes that spreads from itself. A grid makes "spreads from itself" literal and keeps contact checks cheap and exact. The room fills in about 40–45 s. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | A prototype stone needs fire that burns out, spreads by material, or is blocked by walls. |
| Fire cube presentation | GameObject cubes with one shared emissive URP Lit material, recoloured per cube through a `MaterialPropertyBlock`; variation from a hash of the cell position | Up to about 1,700 cubes is fine as plain GameObjects for a prototype, and a property block avoids one material copy per cube. [MaterialPropertyBlock](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.3/Documentation/ScriptReference/MaterialPropertyBlock.html) | Profiling a standalone build shows the fire display costs noticeable frame time; then move to GPU instancing (`Graphics.RenderMeshInstanced`). |
| Replay compatibility | `SimulationCompatibilityVersion` 5 → 6 and `ContentRevision` 13 → 14 (2026-09-19) for personalities: pace, reaction delay, danger distance, shouting, panic decisions, swerves, hesitation, trips, dodging, ramming, knockdowns and box shoves now depend on traits, and the temperament deck is dealt by fearfulness. Earlier: 4 → 5 and 12 → 13 (2026-09-19; previously 3 → 4 and 11 → 12 for doors and boxes, 2 → 3 and 10 → 11 for temperaments, hearing and collisions) | A body is now moved once per tick. Before, a runner who stopped trying a door had their body moved twice in that tick, so they could turn up to twice their turn rate (24–28° instead of at most 16°, seen with seeds 40 and 46). Events also gained a target ID. Both change replay data, which the [compatibility policy](simulation-contract.md#simulation-compatibility-policy) requires to be versioned. | Any further change to a replay-relevant rule. |
| Code structure (2026-09-19) | The simulation is split into parts that each own their own data. The world geometry (`WorldGeometry`) is the only code that knows the room is a rectangle with doors in its walls. There are separate systems for fire, doors, sound, fear, perception, falls, collisions and boxes, and separate calm, panic and door behaviours. Behaviours only say what the body should do (`MotorIntent`); `Locomotion` is the only code that turns and moves bodies. Each person's record is split into body, personality, fear, intent, hearing and door memory. `FireReactionSimulation` just builds these parts and runs the tick schedule. | Before, one class in nine files shared about 45 fields per person that any file could change. That is how the double-turn bug above happened, and every new stone would have had to touch that shared state. Geometry spread across every file would have made inner walls or a second room a rewrite of most files. | A stone needs inner walls, several rooms or routes: change `WorldGeometry` first. |
| Scenario values (2026-09-19) | Every tunable number lives once, with its default and unit, in a settings group in `ScenarioSettings.cs` (world, perception, fire, steering, calm, panic, temperament, hearing, falls, exits, object physics). The scenario asset stores one block of these groups, and a run works on its own copy. About 40 numbers that were fixed in the code, such as follow strength, door bonuses, stroll distances and steering weights, are now editable in the asset with unchanged values. Only pure mechanics stay fixed in code: sub-millimetre box units, the box contact search steps, and the 75° sharp-turn rule. IDs are called `SimulationId`, because Unity 6.3 already has a type named `EntityId`. | Adding one value used to mean editing four places. Now it is one line. The owner can tune every behaviour number in the Inspector. | Designers need per-scenario presets or curves instead of single numbers. |
| Replay fingerprint (2026-09-19) | `ReplayFingerprintEditModeTests` runs the default scenario for 60 s with seeds 42, 40 and 46, both with every door locked and with two doors opened. It also runs two cases with every box kicked toward the middle. Each run is squashed into one number: every person's position and state on every tick, every event, the final boxes and the random generator's state. A restructure must keep every number. A deliberate behaviour change re-records them and bumps the compatibility version. | This proves a code change is invisible to players, which the owner cannot check by eye. The step-by-step restructure above kept all fingerprints identical. The box cases were added because the default runs contain few box-on-box hits. | A new stone changes behaviour on purpose; re-record in the same commit. |
| Fire queries and snapshots (2026-09-19) | Questions such as "nearest fire", "fire within X" and "can I see fire" only look at grid cells within reach. When fewer cells are burning than it would have to look at, the check falls back to testing every burning cell. Answers, ties included, match checking every cell; a test compares them at 1,681 points for small, medium and large fires. Snapshots copy people, doors and boxes. They only hold a view of the event log and the burning cells, which never shrink. | Before, one panic decision checked about 25,000 cells once the room was alight, and every tick copied the whole event history. Both costs grew with fire size and run length. | Profiling shows people-to-people checks are the cost, for example with many more people; then add a grid for people too. |
| Display structure (2026-09-19) | The display is split into room and doors, people, boxes, fire, ripples, on-screen text and door-click input, all under one "Fire reaction presentation" object. Events name what they affected (`TargetId`), so a kicked box and a shoved door are found by ID instead of "whichever is nearest". Colours use one shared `MaterialPropertyBlock`, and every material is destroyed when the display goes away. The runner creates its run the first time something asks for it, so the display does not depend on Unity's start-up order. | Guessing the nearest box could make the wrong one hop, and materials leaked on every scene reload. | Icons or effects need authored art or pooling. |
| Doors (2026-09-18) | One 1.0 m door in every wall, set 2.5 m off-centre in a pinwheel (north at x = −2.5 m, east at z = +2.5 m, south at x = +2.5 m, west at z = −2.5 m). All start locked. | The owner asked for a door in every wall. 1 m is an ordinary single door: one person at a time, so queues and doorway crushes appear by themselves. Off-centre doors give each corner of the room a different nearest exit. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | Playtests want wider exits, double doors or authored door layouts per scenario. |
| Door clicks (2026-09-18) | Locked → click → unlocked (green) → click → open. A click on an open door does nothing. Clicks are `ClickDoor` player commands for the next tick. Unity's Input System reads the mouse; a ray-cast against a click-only collider on each door leaf finds which door was clicked. | The owner specified unlock-then-open. Closing a door on someone standing in the doorway needs rules the prototype does not have yet, so open is final for now. The Input System is already installed and set as the only input backend; the collider only identifies the door and never decides anything in the simulation. [Input System mouse](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.inputsystem@1.16/manual/Mouse.html), [Physics.Raycast](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.3/Documentation/ScriptReference/Physics.Raycast.html) | The player needs to close or re-lock doors, or clicks need to work on touch screens. |
| Escaping (2026-09-18) | An open door adds a 1 m-wide walkable strip from 1 m inside the wall to 2 m outside. Only a person heading for that door (or already outside) may use it. 0.8 m past the wall they are `Escaped`, leave the room and shrink out of view. | "Exit" should mean leaving; the short strip lets the player see them walk out. Calm people keep treating doors as wall, since they are not trying to leave. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | Scenarios need rooms beyond the door, or calm people who leave on their own. |
| Door behaviour (2026-09-18) | Runners pick a door by distance, with a 5 m penalty for a route past the fire, an 8 m penalty for a door in the fire, a 4 m bonus if it is visibly open, a 1.5 m bonus for sticking with their current choice, and up to 1.5 m of random noise. At a closed door: 0.4 s to open an unlocked one; 0.5 s rattling a locked one, then 60% shove it every 0.4–0.6 s for 1.5–4 s (a thud others hear), 40% give up at once. Giving up means avoiding that door for 6–12 s and glancing toward the next choice for 0.3–0.6 s. Stuck in a crowd on the way to a door for 12 ticks: avoid it for 2–4 s. If every door is avoided, they fall back to the old "run away from the fire" spot choice. Unlocking a door while someone rattles it makes them open it at once. | The owner asked for people who try doors, sometimes try to force them without success, and look for another way. People can see an open door but not a lock, so red and green are for the player only. Measured headless over seeds 40–46 in the first 20 s with every door locked: 16–29 door attempts, 23–63 shoves, 10–15 give-ups, nobody escapes. With every door open: 8 of 10 escape (the 2 who freeze for good are caught), the first after 1–4 s. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | Playtesters find the rattling too long (shorten forcing) or people too stubborn (shorten the avoid time). |
| Physical objects (2026-09-18) | A physical-object system inside the simulation, not Unity physics: whole-number positions (kept to 1/100 mm), velocities, mass, round footprints the width of the object, spin for looks. Each tick after collisions, every moving object slides, stops at the first person, object or wall in its way, bounces (30% off people and walls, 40% off other objects), loses 1.57 mm/tick of speed per tick to friction (cardboard on concrete, about 0.4), and never goes faster than the 120 mm/tick step limit. Boxes are the only kind: 8 of them, 0.3–0.6 m wide and 3–20 kg. | The owner asked for a foundation for in-world physics objects, starting with boxes. A box can trip a person, so boxes decide outcomes, and the simulation contract forbids Unity physics or floating-point from deciding outcomes. Round footprints are the usual first step. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | Boxes look wrong because corners do not collide, objects need to stack or be carried, or many more objects need profiling. |
| People and boxes (2026-09-18) | A person running into a box pushes it along the line between them as a 70 kg body would. A walker side-steps first and only pushes when boxed in; a runner at 2.5 m/s or more hits it without dodging. At 3 m/s or more there is a chance to trip over it instead: (speed − 3 m/s + 0.5 m/s) × box width ÷ 500, capped at 75%, so about 50% for a 0.6 m box at 4.5 m/s and about 25% for a 0.3 m box. A sliding box into a person: momentum ≥ 800 kg·mm/tick staggers them, ≥ 1,600 trips them; below that it just bounces off. Calm people steer well clear of boxes; runners only a little. | The owner asked for boxes that bump around and sometimes trip people, depending on size and speed. Measured headless over seeds 40–46 in the first 20 s with doors locked: 5–24 box bumps, 0–5 box trips, 0–2 people hit by flying boxes. Chosen on the owner's behalf. | Box trips feel too common or too rare in play; change the 500 scale or the 75% cap first. |
| Personalities (2026-09-19) | Six traits per person, 0–10, with 5 an ordinary person who behaves exactly as the other settings say. The default ten are an authored cast (brute, hero, saint, villain, nervous wreck, sprinter, bully, coward and two ordinary people); people without authored traits get the rounded average of two seeded 0–10 draws, so 5 is common and 0 or 10 rare. Effects, per point away from 5: strength ±10% box-shoving weight, and 4+ points stronger only staggers in a knock-down collision; bravery −10% reaction delay and −5% danger distance; nervousness −10% shout and panic-decision intervals, +5 points swerve chance, +2 points hesitation chance, +10% trip chance; compassion +15% and evil −15% people-avoidance while running, and compassion +5 and evil −5 mm/tick on the speed at which a runner rams rather than dodges (never below 20). Freezing temperaments go to the most fearful (nervousness minus bravery), seed breaking ties. Shown as a number beside each head and a Tab table. | The owner asked for traits that strongly shape behaviour and, later, can be changed by the player. An authored cast guarantees every personality appears in every run, the lesson of the temperament deck. "5 = unchanged" keeps every earlier tuning valid. The owner chose numbers on screen plus a toggled table to keep play clean. Measured headless, seeds 40–46, first 20 s, doors locked: 9–31 collisions, 1–4 knockdowns (4–12 before: strong people shrug hits off and compassionate people dodge), 1–7 trips; all doors open: 6–8 of 10 escape. Chosen on the owner's behalf except the display. | Traits feel invisible in play (raise the per-point percentages), or knockdowns feel too rare for later stones that build on them (lower `StrengthShrugOffGap` effect or knockdown speed). |
| Prototype presentation camera | Orthographic camera at a 45-degree horizontal angle and 35.264-degree elevation | This is the familiar isometric game view and keeps the whole 12 m room readable. | Playtesting shows that depth or agent readability requires a different camera. |

## How decisions are made

Paniq's owner is learning game development, so technical decisions must remain
teachable and reversible. Before adding a package, workflow, or major system:

1. Research the current official documentation and inspect the existing project.
2. State the problem in game terms, then give one plain-language recommendation.
3. Record why it is needed now, what simpler option was retained or rejected,
   relevant source links, and the condition that would trigger a later review.
4. Explain the implementation result and exact local Unity steps needed to
   verify it.

Ask the owner only to choose genuine product direction, such as the intended
player experience or visual tone - not engine settings they have no reason to
know yet.

## Design constraints for future expansion

The authoritative rules for replayable simulation are in
[Simulation contract](simulation-contract.md). They apply before any gameplay
system is introduced.

- Keep interaction data explicit: source ID, event type, world position,
  strength, duration, and causal parent.
- Keep configuration in scenes and ScriptableObjects rather than hidden static
  state.
- Keep random choices tied to an explicit scenario seed.
- Keep presentation separate from future gameplay/simulation decisions.
- Prefer simple steering and authored obstacles before adding navigation.

## Deferred technology

| Technology | Do not add it until |
| --- | --- |
| ECS, Burst, Entities Graphics | A profiled representative prototype scene cannot meet its frame-rate target because of crowd update or rendering cost. |
| AI Navigation | A prototype stone needs pathfinding that simple steering and obstacle avoidance cannot provide. |
| Cinemachine | The hand-authored camera prevents a required player experience. |
| Addressables | Content size, loading needs, or platform packaging makes direct references difficult to manage. |
| Mobile settings | The Windows prototype is stable and a target device is available for measurement. |

Any addition above requires a short dated note here recording the measured or
feature-driven reason and the expected benefit.
